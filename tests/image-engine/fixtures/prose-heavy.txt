Most people think they fail at building habits because they lack willpower. I used to believe that too, and I spent years blaming myself every time a new routine fell apart after a few weeks. But willpower is a terrible thing to build a life on. It runs out when you are tired, stressed or distracted, which is exactly when a habit is supposed to carry you.

The real problem is that we design habits for the person we wish we were, not the person we are on a bad day. We pick a big, shiny goal, make a plan that only works when everything goes right, and then feel like failures when a normal, messy week gets in the way. The plan was never built for real life, so of course it broke.

What actually works is almost embarrassingly small. A habit sticks when it is so easy that you can do it even when you are exhausted, and when it is attached to something you already do without thinking. If you want to read more, you do not set out to finish a book. You leave the book on your pillow and read a single page before you sleep. The goal is not progress on the first day. The goal is to make the action automatic, because once it is automatic you can grow it without a fight.

Environment matters more than motivation, too. People who stick with habits quietly arrange their surroundings so the good choice is the easy one and the bad choice takes effort. They put the running shoes by the door and the phone in another room. They stop asking whether they feel like it, because they have already removed the decision.

Finally, be kind to yourself when you miss a day. The people who build lasting habits are not the ones who never slip. They are the ones who treat a missed day as information, not as a verdict, and who simply start again the next morning without a speech about what it says about them.
